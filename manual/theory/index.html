Theoretical basis

Theoretical basis

What the core computes and how, core 0.7.0. Each equation is written in the code's convention, cites its source, and names the function that implements it. These entries are also what the app's tooltips and inspector help will say (APP_CONVENTIONS.md §5): a statistic's help is never typed in the app. The course's own guides are the first source: Cálculo de errores en las medidas (errores.pdf) and Regresiones lineales mediante ordenador (regresiones.pdf), in practices/uned/tecI/resources/.

Writing a result

A measured quantity is written \(x \pm \Delta x\) with its unit. The error is given with one significant figure, exceptionally two when the first is 1 ("una cifra y media"); the value is rounded to the error's last place. errores.pdf, p. 1 · stats::written

Repeated readings

For \(n\) readings \(x_1 \dots x_n\) of one quantity, the best value is the mean and its error the "error cuadrático":

\(\bar x = \frac{1}{n}\sum_k x_k, \qquad \Delta \bar x = \sqrt{\frac{\sum_k (\bar x - x_k)^2}{n(n-1)}}\)

which is the standard error of the mean (68 % within \(\pm\Delta\bar x\) under Gauss's assumptions). It is taken as the error only when it exceeds the instrument's resolution (its smallest division), which is otherwise the error. The sample standard deviation \(s = \sqrt{\sum_k (\bar x - x_k)^2/(n-1)}\) is reported beside it. errores.pdf, pp. 2–3, eqs. 1–2 · stats::repeats; in a formula, mean(…) and sem(…)

Propagation of errors

For \(y = f(p, q, r, \dots)\) with errors \(\Delta p, \Delta q, \dots\), by quadrature (equation 3 of the course's guide):

\(\Delta y = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial p}\Delta p\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial q}\Delta q\right)^2 + \cdots}\)

or by the upper bound (equation 4), larger, used for quick estimates and by several of the practices' guides for a one-variable result (\(\Delta g = |4\pi^2/m^2|\,\Delta m\)):

\((\Delta y)_{\max} = \left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial p}\right|\Delta p + \left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial q}\right|\Delta q + \cdots\)

The partial derivatives are taken numerically by central differences at the measured values, so that any formula can be propagated; the contribution \(|\partial f/\partial p|\,\Delta p\) of each error is reported, which says which error dominates. errores.pdf, p. 3, eqs. 3–4 · propagate::quadrature, propagate::upper_bound, formula::apply

The straight line

For \(N\) points \((x_i, y_i)\), the line \(y = m x + n\) by least squares, with \(\Delta = N\sum x_i^2 - (\sum x_i)^2\):

\(m = \frac{N\sum x_i y_i - \sum x_i \sum y_i}{\Delta}, \qquad n = \frac{\sum x_i^2 \sum y_i - \sum x_i \sum x_i y_i}{\Delta}\)

The errors of the parameters from the scatter of the points about the line, with the residual variance \(s^2 = \sum (y_i - m x_i - n)^2/(N-2)\):

\(\Delta m = s\sqrt{\frac{N}{\Delta}}, \qquad \Delta n = s\sqrt{\frac{\sum x_i^2}{\Delta}}\)

This is the convention of SciPy's linregress and of KaleidaGraph's fit1, the fit the course's regression guide uses: confirmed on the demo with the Hooke data to every printed digit (L023, test kaleidagraph). Through the origin: \(m = \sum x_i y_i / \sum x_i^2\), \(s^2 = \sum (y_i - m x_i)^2/(N-1)\), \(\Delta m = s/\sqrt{\sum x_i^2}\). Pearson's \(r\) and \(R^2 = r^2\) are reported, and the correlation between \(m\) and \(n\). regresiones.pdf · linear::fit_line; test hooke against SciPy

Weighted fits and the three conventions for the errors

With a known error \(\sigma_i\) on each \(y_i\), the weights are \(w_i = 1/\sigma_i^2\) and the fit minimises \(\chi^2 = \sum w_i (y_i - f(x_i))^2\). The parameters' errors come from the inverse of the normal matrix \(A = J^\mathsf{T} W J\) (\(J\) the Jacobian of \(f\) with respect to the parameters). Every result names which convention it used:

  • scatter (unweighted): \(\sigma_j = \sqrt{(A^{-1})_{jj}\,\chi^2/\nu}\), \(\nu = N - p\) the degrees of freedom; the errors follow the points' scatter, as the line's \(\Delta m\), \(\Delta n\) above.
  • weights: \(\sigma_j = \sqrt{(A^{-1})_{jj}}\); the errors follow the \(\sigma_i\) given, whatever the scatter (SciPy's absolute_sigma=True). Then \(\chi^2/\nu\) tells whether the \(\sigma_i\) were honest: near 1 if they were.
  • weights scaled: the former times \(\sqrt{\chi^2/\nu}\) (SciPy's default, absolute_sigma=False); with a constant \(\sigma\) this equals the scatter convention.

linear::fit_line, nonlinear::fit_compiled, poly::fit_polynomial; tests hooke, fits

The general fit

For any equation \(y = f(x; p_1 \dots p_k)\), the parameters that minimise \(\chi^2\) are found by Gauss–Newton steps from the starting values: at each step the normal matrix \(A = J^\mathsf{T} W J\) and the gradient \(g = J^\mathsf{T} W (y - f)\) are formed, \(A\) is scaled to unit diagonal, \(\hat A = D^{-1} A D^{-1}\), and decomposed into its eigenvectors, \(\hat A = V \Lambda V^\mathsf{T}\); the step is

\(\delta = D^{-1} V (\Lambda + \lambda)^{-1} V^\mathsf{T} D^{-1} g\)

with \(\lambda = 0\) (Gauss–Newton) unless the step would raise \(\chi^2\), in which case \(\lambda = 10^{-3}\) and \(\times 10\) until it falls (Marquardt's damping), ÷10 on each accepted step. A direction with \(\Lambda_k < 10^{-12}\Lambda_{\max}\) is singular: the data cannot fix that combination of parameters, which is named in the result and left out of the step. A parameter with bounds is held at its bound while the gradient pushes it out, and freed when it pulls in. The fit converges when every computed shift is below \(10^{-10}\) of its parameter, or \(\chi^2\) no longer changes. The Jacobian is taken by central differences, since the equation is the user's text. The covariance is \(A^{-1}\) under the three conventions above; the correlations \(\rho_{jk} = C_{jk}/\sqrt{C_{jj}C_{kk}}\). The method of diff-ref-struct's engine (its lsq.rs, after FullProf's manual eqs. 3.5–3.8), written for small problems (L017) · solve::solve_damped, nonlinear::fit_compiled; test fits against SciPy's curve_fit

KaleidaGraph's standard fits: Exponential, Logarithmic, Power

KaleidaGraph's "Least Squares" fits of \(y = m_0 e^{m_1 x}\), \(y = m_0 + m_1 \log x\) and \(y = m_0 x^{m_1}\) can give results "slightly different" from the General fit of the same equation and show no parameter errors (manual, p. 151): they are fitted as straight lines to the transformed data, \(\ln y\) against \(x\), \(y\) against \(\log_{10} x\), \(\ln y\) against \(\ln x\), which weights each point by its size (a point with a large \(y\) counts less in \(\ln y\)). The core does the same (method linearised), with the errors of \(m_0\) and \(m_1\) propagated from the line's (\(\Delta m_0 = m_0 \Delta n\) for the exponential and power fits) and marked as such; and it fits the same equation by the general fit (method nonlinear), the true least squares on the original data, started from the linearised solution, which always has the lower \(\chi^2\). \(R\) is Pearson's correlation between the fitted curve and the data, as KaleidaGraph reports it for the Power fit (p. 174). The base of the Logarithmic fit's logarithm is taken as 10, KaleidaGraph's log: to be confirmed on the demo. KaleidaGraph Manual, pp. 151–154, 174 · presets::fit_preset; test presets against NumPy and SciPy

The Linear fit's statistics and the bands

What KaleidaGraph's View Results lists for a Linear fit (p. 175), with \(\nu = N - 2\): the adjusted \(R^2_{\mathrm{adj}} = 1 - (1 - R^2)(N-1)/\nu\); the standard error of the estimate \(s\); the \(t\) values \(t_m = m/\Delta m\), \(t_n = n/\Delta n\) and their two-sided \(P\) values from Student's \(t\) with \(\nu\) degrees of freedom; \(F = R^2 / ((1-R^2)/\nu)\) and its \(P\) value from Fisher's \(F(1, \nu)\); the confidence limits \(m \pm t_{\nu}(p)\,\Delta m\), \(n \pm t_{\nu}(p)\,\Delta n\) at the level \(p\) (95 % by default). None of them through the origin, as KaleidaGraph. The confidence band of the line at \(x\) is \(\hat y \pm t_{\nu}(p)\, s \sqrt{1/N + (x-\bar x)^2/S_{xx}}\) with \(S_{xx} = \sum (x_i - \bar x)^2\); the prediction band for a new observation has \(1 + 1/N + \cdots\) under the root. The \(t\) and \(F\) distributions are computed through the regularised incomplete beta function. KaleidaGraph Manual, pp. 175, 180 · linear::statistics, linear::band, dist; test presets against SciPy

The polynomial

\(y = c_0 + c_1 x + \cdots + c_k x^k\) to order 9 is linear in its coefficients: one solve of the normal equations on the design matrix of the powers of \((x - \bar x)/x_s\) (centred and scaled for conditioning), the coefficients and their covariance mapped back to the powers of \(x\) by the binomial theorem. The errors follow the three conventions. poly::fit_polynomial; test fits against NumPy's lstsq

Measurement uncertainty by the GUM, and Monte Carlo

The GUM uncertainty framework (JCGM 100:2008). Each input quantity \(x_i\) has a standard uncertainty \(u(x_i)\): by a Type A evaluation, from \(n\) readings, \(u = s/\sqrt{n}\) with \(\nu_i = n - 1\) degrees of freedom (clause 4.2); or by a Type B evaluation, from other knowledge: a Gaussian with a stated \(u\); a rectangular distribution of half-width \(a\), \(u = a/\sqrt 3\) (4.3.7); a triangular one, \(u = a/\sqrt 6\) (4.3.9); the resolution \(\delta\) of an indicating instrument, rectangular of half-width \(\delta/2\), \(u = \delta/\sqrt{12}\) (F.2.2.1). For \(y = f(x_1, \dots, x_N)\) with uncorrelated inputs, the law of propagation of uncertainty (5.1.2):

\(u_c^2(y) = \sum_i c_i^2\, u^2(x_i), \qquad c_i = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\)

the sensitivity coefficients taken by central differences at the estimates; the uncertainty budget lists each contribution \(c_i u(x_i)\) and its share of \(u_c^2\). The effective degrees of freedom by Welch–Satterthwaite (G.4.1), with \(\nu_i = \infty\) for a Type B evaluation:

\(\nu_{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{u_c^4(y)}{\sum_i c_i^4 u^4(x_i)/\nu_i}\)

The coverage factor \(k = t_{\nu_{\mathrm{eff}}}(p)\) from Student's \(t\) at the coverage probability \(p\) (G.3; \(k \to 1.96\) at 95 % for infinite \(\nu\), 2 at 95.45 %); the expanded uncertainty \(U = k\, u_c(y)\) and the coverage interval \(y \pm U\) (6.2). JCGM 100:2008, clauses 4, 5, 6, G · gum::framework, gum::coverage_factor; test gum

The Monte Carlo method (JCGM 101:2008, Supplement 1). The inputs are sampled \(M\) times from their distributions (a Type A input as \(\bar x + (s/\sqrt n)\, t_{n-1}\), 6.4.9.2; a rectangular, triangular or Gaussian as stated), the model is evaluated at each draw, and the \(M\) values give the estimate (their average), \(u(y)\) (their standard deviation) and the coverage interval at \(p\): the probabilistically symmetric one, between the \((1-p)/2\) and \((1+p)/2\) quantiles, and the shortest one (7.7). The draws come from a deterministic generator with a seed, so a report can be reproduced. Validation (clause 8): with \(u(y)\) written to \(n_{\mathrm{dig}}\) significant digits as \(c \times 10^l\), the tolerance is \(\delta = \tfrac12 \times 10^l\); the framework is validated when both endpoints of \(y \pm U\) lie within \(\delta\) of the Monte Carlo interval's. When the model is far from linear over the inputs' spread, or an input's distribution is far from Gaussian, the framework fails the validation and the Monte Carlo result is the one to report; \(y = x^2\) with \(x \sim N(0, 1)\) is the test's example, where the law of propagation gives \(u_c = 0\). JCGM 101:2008, clauses 6, 7, 8 · gum::monte_carlo, gum::validate, rng; test gum

The course's rules in the GUM's terms. The "error cuadrático" of \(n\) readings is the Type A standard uncertainty. The instrument's resolution taken as the error of a single reading is a Type B evaluation; the GUM's standard uncertainty for it is \(\delta/\sqrt{12}\), about 0.29 \(\delta\), smaller than the \(\delta\) the course writes. The quadrature of equation 3 is the law of propagation for uncorrelated inputs; the upper bound of equation 4 has no GUM counterpart: it is a worst case, not a standard uncertainty. The course's \(\pm \Delta x\) is a standard uncertainty (\(k = 1\)), not an expanded one; a report written by the GUM states \(U\), \(k\) and \(p\). The app's fit report can be written either way; the template (R13) says which. stats::repeats, propagate, gum

Units and dimensions

A unit is a factor to the SI base and a vector of exponents over (m, kg, s, A, K, mol, cd). A formula is evaluated in SI; its unit follows the rules: a sum or a difference needs equal dimensions, a product or quotient adds or subtracts the exponents, a power multiplies them by a plain number, \(\sqrt{\,}\) halves them, any other function wants a dimensionless argument. The result is converted to the unit asked for when the dimensions agree, and refused otherwise. units, expr::Compiled::unit_of, formula::apply; test formulas

A reading far from the fit

After a fit, each residual \(r_i = y_i - f(x_i)\) is compared with the residual standard deviation \(s = \sqrt{\sum r_i^2 / \nu}\), \(\nu\) the degrees of freedom: a reading with \(|r_i| > 3s\) is marked as far from the fit. For Gaussian scatter that happens to fewer than three readings in a thousand, so the mark is a suggestion to remeasure, which is what the Bench shows the student, never a refusal and never a dialog: an outlier may be real, and the decision is the student's. the course's practice of repeating a doubtful reading · fitfile::run, Residual::sigmas, Residual::far

Confidence and prediction bands of a straight line

For the line \(y = m x + n\) fitted to \(N\) points with residual standard deviation \(s\), the fitted value at \(x_0\) has standard error \(s\sqrt{1/N + (x_0 - \bar x)^2 / S_{xx}}\), and the confidence band at level \(1-\alpha\) is the fitted value \(\pm\, t_{\alpha/2,\,N-2}\) times that error: where the true line lies. The prediction band adds the scatter of one new reading, \(s\sqrt{1 + 1/N + (x_0 - \bar x)^2 / S_{xx}}\): where a new reading would fall. Both are narrowest at \(\bar x\) and widen away from it. KaleidaGraph's Linear fit, View Results · linear::band, drawn by the plot through plot::CurveOut::band

Axes and ticks

An axis is drawn so that its labels never overlap or crowd (APP_CONVENTIONS.md §8; the lesson of diff-ref-struct's L119). For a data range [lo, hi] along L pixels, the number of labels is n = ⌊L / s⌋, with s the room one label needs (70 px along x, 32 along y), between 2 and 12; the step is the smallest of 1, 2, \(5 \times 10\)k at or above (hi − lo)/n; the axis runs from ⌊lo/step⌋·step to ⌈hi/step⌉·step, so that both ends are ticks; each tick's position is its index over the number of steps, exact. When the largest tick is 104 or more, or below 10−3, the labels are divided by 10e, e = ⌊log10 max|tick|⌋, and "\(\times 10\)e" goes into the axis title; when the range is less than a thousandth of the values (10.6048 … 10.6052), the first tick is subtracted from the labels and "+ 10.6048" goes into the title. Labels show the digits the step needs and no more, with the true minus sign (U+2212) and never "−0". A log axis ticks the decades 10k between ⌊log10 lo⌋ and ⌈log10 hi⌉, every other decade when they are many, and adds 2 and 5 between them when there are two decades or fewer. The map from a fraction t of the axis to a value is a + b·t on a linear axis and 10a + b·t on a log one; the window uses it, and nothing else, to read values under the cursor. Implemented in axis.rs (linear, log), tested in tests/plot.rs.
